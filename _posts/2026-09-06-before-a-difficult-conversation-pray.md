A difficult conversation can occupy the mind long before it happens. We rehearse sentences, predict objections and build a case against the other person. Prayer does not guarantee an easy outcome. It can help us enter the room less controlled by fear, pride or the need to win.

This is especially fitting on 6 September 2026. The [liturgical calendar](https://www.liturgyoffice.org.uk/Calendar/2026/Sep.shtml) marks it as the Twenty-Third Sunday in Ordinary Time, and the Year A readings address responsibility, love and speaking directly within a community.

## Pray before rehearsing your case

Preparation is wise, but anxious rehearsal can harden a conversation before the other person has spoken. We assign motives, imagine the worst reply and sharpen phrases that may never be needed.

Place the Rosary in your hand before writing the script in your head. Name the person and ask God to protect both of you from contempt. Notice what your body is doing. If your jaw is tight or your breathing is shallow, slow down. Physical tension can turn even accurate words into an attack.

Prayer creates a pause in which the goal can change from defeating an opponent to seeking truth and the good of the relationship.

## Name the good you are seeking

“I need to say something” is not yet a clear purpose. Ask what you hope the conversation will make possible. Do you need to correct inaccurate information, address hurt, set a boundary, repair trust or agree on a practical next step?

Write the purpose in one sentence. If it depends entirely on controlling the other person’s reaction, revise it. You can choose honesty, timing and tone. You cannot force agreement, remorse or affection.

A worthy aim might be: “I want to explain the impact of what happened and ask for a different arrangement.” That is more useful than “I want them to admit I am right.”

## Separate facts from interpretations

Conflict grows when assumptions are presented as evidence. List what you directly know: the words said, the decision made, the deadline missed or the behaviour repeated. Then list what you inferred about motive.

Your interpretation may be correct, but hold it with humility. Instead of saying, “You do not care,” describe the event and ask a question. “When I did not hear from you, I felt abandoned. Can you tell me what happened?” gives the other person room to supply information.

This distinction does not weaken truth. It makes truth more precise. It also reduces the chance that the whole conversation will become a defence against an accusation you could not prove.

## Choose words that preserve dignity

The [Liturgy Office’s page for the Twenty-Third Sunday](https://www.liturgyoffice.org.uk/Calendar/Sunday/OT4Sunday.shtml) identifies Matthew 18:15–20 as the Year A Gospel and Romans 13:8–10 as the second reading. The readings place direct speech within the larger demand of love.

Love does not require avoiding a serious issue. It asks us to speak without humiliation. Address the matter with the person concerned before widening the audience, unless safety, safeguarding or formal reporting requires another route. Avoid collecting allies simply to make the other person feel surrounded.

Choose one example rather than unloading every grievance in the relationship. Do not use confidential pain as a weapon. If anger is too strong for respectful speech, arrange a later time instead of pretending you are ready.

## Listen without surrendering the truth

Listening is not agreement. You can hear the other person’s account, recognise a valid point and still maintain a necessary boundary.

Ask them to explain how they saw the situation. Do not interrupt the first difficult sentence. When they finish, summarise the part you understood before giving your response. This helps both people identify the real disagreement.

If the conversation becomes abusive, threatening or unsafe, end it and seek appropriate support. Prayer is not a command to remain exposed to harm. Courage may mean continuing the discussion; it may also mean leaving, documenting what happened or involving someone responsible.

## Leave room for an unfinished ending

Some conversations reach a clear agreement. Others end with more work to do. Do not force a false resolution because discomfort is hard to bear.

State what has been agreed, what remains unresolved and when you will review it. If you owe an apology, make it specific and do not attach an excuse. If forgiveness is sought, remember that rebuilding trust may still require time, evidence and changed behaviour.

Afterwards, pray again. Resist the urge to retell the exchange in a way that only confirms your innocence. Ask where you spoke well, where repair is needed and what responsibility now belongs to you.

Before today’s hardest conversation, hold your Rosary and pray one Our Father for the other person’s good. Write your purpose, one fact you need to address and one question you genuinely need answered. Then speak the truth without treating dignity as a negotiable extra.
