Leadership often rewards the person who answers fastest. A listening heart follows a different order: receive what is true, pray for wisdom, and then act with courage. This matters in a workplace, a parish, a household and any relationship where our decisions affect somebody else.

On 3 September, the Church in England celebrates St Gregory the Great. His life offers a helpful lens for considering leadership that is active without becoming self-important.

## A pastor in a demanding time

The [Liturgy Office’s National Calendar for England](https://www.liturgyoffice.org.uk/Calendar/National/England2.shtml) recalls that Gregory lived from about 540 to 604, served as prefect of Rome, entered monastic life and was elected pope in 590. It also remembers his work in Church administration, pastoral writing and the mission to the English.

That is a life of substantial responsibility. Yet the lasting interest is not simply the number of tasks he completed. Gregory’s pastoral concern points towards a form of authority that asks what people need in order to grow in faith.

Most of us will never hold public office in the Church. We may still supervise a team, care for children, coordinate volunteers or influence a friend. Gregory’s day can prompt an honest question: does my responsibility make me more attentive to people, or only more attached to being right?

## Listen before deciding what the problem is

Poor listening often starts with premature certainty. We hear the first sentence and assume we know the rest. We prepare a solution while the other person is still explaining, or fit their concern into a category that is convenient for us.

Listening requires more than silence. It means asking a useful question, allowing an answer to complicate our first impression and noticing what has not yet been said. It also means checking facts. Compassion without accuracy can still lead to a harmful decision.

Try reflecting the concern back in your own words: “Have I understood that this is the part causing the difficulty?” A short check can prevent a long misunderstanding.

## Let prayer interrupt urgency

Some decisions genuinely cannot wait. Many only feel urgent because our discomfort wants a fast ending. Prayer creates enough space to distinguish the two.

Before responding, hold the Rosary and name the people affected. Ask for freedom from defensiveness, fear and the desire to look impressive. The repeated prayers can slow the interior rush that turns every disagreement into a threat.

This pause is not avoidance. Its purpose is to make action wiser. Once the relevant facts are clear and a decision is needed, prayer should support the courage to make it rather than provide an excuse to delay indefinitely.

## Lead people, not abstractions

Policies, targets and plans matter. They help communities act consistently. But a leader can become so focused on the system that the people inside it seem like interruptions.

A listening heart keeps the human consequences visible. Who will carry the extra work? Who lacks the information needed to succeed? Who is speaking less because previous attempts were dismissed? Who needs a clear boundary rather than another vague reassurance?

This attention does not require agreeing with every request. Sometimes responsible leadership says no. The difference lies in whether the person has been heard, the reason is honest and the decision respects their dignity.

## Speak with clarity and restraint

After listening, leadership has to communicate. Vague language may feel kinder in the moment, but it can leave people anxious or unable to act. Say what has been decided, why it matters and what happens next.

Restraint matters too. There is rarely a need to expose every frustration, repeat confidential details or use authority to win an argument. A clear sentence can carry more care than a long defence of your intentions.

If correction is needed, address the action rather than reducing the person to their mistake. If you were wrong, acknowledge it without making others comfort you. Accountability is part of the trust that listening creates.

## Make room for voices unlike your own

We naturally hear people whose style, experience and assumptions resemble ours. Leadership becomes narrower when familiarity is mistaken for wisdom.

Notice whose advice you seek and whose concerns you regularly minimise. A person may communicate hesitantly and still see a risk that everyone else has missed. Another may disagree firmly while remaining committed to the same good.

Listening across difference does not mean abandoning judgement. It gives judgement better material. Prayer can help us receive a challenging voice without deciding in advance that challenge equals disloyalty.

## Practise one attentive meeting

St Gregory’s memorial is not only a day to admire leadership from the past. It can reshape one encounter today. Choose a conversation in which you hold some responsibility. Put the phone away, ask one question you do not already know the answer to, and let the other person finish.

Before the conversation, pray an Our Father while holding your Rosary. Afterwards, write down the concern you heard and the action you owe. A listening heart becomes credible when attention is followed by faithful responsibility.
