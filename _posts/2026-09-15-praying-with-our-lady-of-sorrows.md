When someone you love is in pain, the wish to fix it can be almost as strong as the sorrow itself. Yet grief, illness, heartbreak and fear do not always yield to advice. The memorial of Our Lady of Sorrows gives us Mary standing near Christ in his suffering: loving him, unable to prevent the Cross and refusing to abandon him.

This is not a picture of helplessness made holy. It is a witness to faithful presence when control is gone. The Rosary can help us stay near another person without making promises we cannot keep or explanations they did not ask for.

## Stand with Mary at the cross

The Gospel of John places Mary near the cross with other faithful women and the beloved disciple. In its reflection on the [Memorial of Our Lady of Sorrows](https://www.vaticannews.va/en/liturgical-holidays/memorial-of-our-lady-of-sorrows.html), Vatican News emphasises that Jesus entrusts Mary and the disciple to one another. Even at the Cross, relationship and care are not forgotten.

Mary’s presence does not remove her Son’s pain. Nor does her sorrow indicate weak faith. She stays as mother and disciple, holding love and anguish together. That makes her a companion for anyone who has sat beside a hospital bed, waited through a crisis or watched someone grieve.

During the Crucifixion mystery, place the person you love beside Mary in your intention. You do not need to imagine their pain in detail. Simply ask that they be held in Christ’s love and surrounded by trustworthy care.

## Release the need to explain

Pain often attracts sentences that are meant to comfort but land as judgement: everything happens for a reason, faith should make this easier or prayer will soon take it away. Such claims can isolate the person who is hurting.

The Rosary gives you words without requiring a theory. You can pray the familiar prayers and allow unanswered questions to remain unanswered. Lament, tears and even numbness have a place before God.

When speaking with the person, use plain language: “I am sorry,” “I am here,” or “Would you like company?” Ask before offering advice. Do not compare their pain with someone else’s story or insist on identifying a spiritual lesson.

## Let each sorrow make room for someone

Devotion to Our Lady of Sorrows is closely associated with Mary’s seven sorrows, from Simeon’s prophecy to the burial of Jesus. You can use that pattern to pray for different forms of suffering without ranking them.

Remember parents afraid for a child, families forced from home, people searching for someone missing, those watching a loved one be mistreated, people present at a death, and those living through burial and its aftermath. Include grief that others may not see or understand.

Keep real people ahead of categories. If a name comes to mind, hold it with care and confidentiality. The purpose is not to gather as much sorrow as possible, but to entrust people to God without looking away.

## Offer presence in a practical form

Companionship becomes credible through specific help. Instead of saying “Let me know if you need anything,” offer something the person can accept or decline: a meal on Tuesday, a lift to an appointment, childcare for an hour or a call at a stated time.

Follow their lead. Some people want to talk; others need help with ordinary tasks. Do not share updates without permission. Remember important dates after the first rush of support has passed.

There will also be needs beyond your ability. Help connect the person with medical, bereavement, safeguarding, financial or pastoral support when appropriate. Accompaniment includes recognising when qualified care is needed.

## Do not measure grief by visible faith

A person can trust God and still feel devastated, angry, confused or exhausted. Prayer may bring consolation, but it does not always change pain immediately. Do not evaluate someone’s faith by whether they cry, struggle to pray or seem hopeful on a particular day.

If your own prayer feels empty, let the beads give your hands a task while the Church’s words carry you. You may sit with the Rosary without completing every prayer. What matters here is not spiritual performance but honest presence before God.

If grief is making daily life unmanageable, or if there is concern about safety, seek professional help. Spiritual support and clinical care can stand alongside each other.

## Receive care as well as giving it

Accompanying another person can be tiring, especially over weeks or months. Mary is not alone at Calvary; others are present, and Jesus creates a bond of care between her and the disciple. You also need people with whom responsibility can be shared.

Ask a family member, parish contact or professional service what support is available. Keep appropriate boundaries and rest. Receiving help does not make your love less faithful; it may allow care to continue without resentment or collapse.

Tonight, place a Rosary beside a flower or safely lit candle. Pray the Sorrowful Mysteries for one person whose pain you cannot fix. Afterwards, send a short message that asks no explanation: tell them you are thinking of them and offer one specific form of help. If they decline, respect the answer and keep the door open through patient, reliable care.
